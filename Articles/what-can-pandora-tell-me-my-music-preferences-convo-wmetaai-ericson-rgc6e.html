You can patent a taxonomy?

Yes, it is possible to patent a taxonomy, but with certain limitations and nuances.

Patenting Taxonomies

Utility Patents

In the United States, utility patents can be granted for novel and non-obvious taxonomies that provide a practical application or solve a specific problem.

Method Claims

Taxonomies can be patented as part of a larger method or system, such as a music recommendation algorithm.

Data Structures

Taxonomies can also be patented as data structures, which are considered a type of software invention.

Limitations

Abstract Ideas

Taxonomies that are purely abstract or conceptual may not be eligible for patent protection.

Prior Art

If a taxonomy is based on existing knowledge or prior art, it may not be considered novel or non-obvious.

Obviousness

If a taxonomy is simply a rearrangement of existing concepts, it may be considered obvious and not eligible for patent protection.

Examples

Pandora's Music Genome Project

Pandora's music taxonomy is patented as part of their music recommendation system.

Google's Knowledge Graph

Google's Knowledge Graph, a massive taxonomy of entities and relationships, is patented as a data structure.

IBM's Watson

IBM's Watson, a question-answering system, uses a patented taxonomy to organize and retrieve knowledge.

Controversies

Patent Trolls

Some companies have been accused of patent trolling, using overly broad taxonomy patents to sue other companies.

Limiting Innovation

Overly broad or vague taxonomy patents can stifle innovation and limit the development of new technologies.

Alternatives

Open-Source Taxonomies

Many taxonomies are developed and shared openly, without patent protection.

Standards Organizations

Industry standards organizations, like the W3C, develop and maintain taxonomies for specific domains.

Public Domain

Some taxonomies are released into the public domain, allowing anyone to use and build upon them.

In summary, while it is possible to patent a taxonomy, the process is complex, and the scope of protection is limited. The patentability of taxonomies depends on their novelty, non-obviousness, and practical application.

Do other streaming services have patented recommender systems, and if so, how do they differ from Netflix in order not to be infringing?

Other streaming services, like Amazon Prime and Disney+, have developed their own patented recommender systems to provide personalized content suggestions to users. While these systems share similarities with Netflix's patented technology, they differ in key aspects to avoid infringement.

Key differences:

  • Algorithmic approaches: Each streaming service uses unique algorithms to analyze user behavior, content attributes, and other factors to generate recommendations. For instance, Amazon Prime's recommender system incorporates natural language processing and collaborative filtering, whereas Disney+ focuses on content-based filtering and knowledge graph-based recommendations.

  • Data sources: Streaming services utilize different data sources, such as user ratings, watch history, search queries, and social media interactions, to inform their recommender systems. This variation in data sources helps differentiate their systems from Netflix's.

  • Weighting and ranking: Each service employs distinct weighting and ranking mechanisms to prioritize content recommendations. For example, Amazon Prime might prioritize recommendations based on user purchase history, while Disney+ focuses on content popularity and user engagement.

  • Content categorization: Streaming services categorize content differently, using unique taxonomies and metadata structures. This allows them to create distinct recommender systems that don't infringe on Netflix's patents.

Examples of patented recommender systems:

  • Amazon Prime's US Patent 10,572,833, "Methods and systems for generating personalized content recommendations," focuses on using machine learning and natural language processing to analyze user behavior and content attributes.

  • Disney+'s US Patent 11,046,723, "Content recommendation system and method," emphasizes content-based filtering and knowledge graph-based recommendations.

While these patented systems share similarities with Netflix's technology, their distinct approaches and implementations help them avoid infringement ¹.
