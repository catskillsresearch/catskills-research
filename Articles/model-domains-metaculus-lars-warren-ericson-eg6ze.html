Model domains in Metaculus

Created on 2024-08-31 13:02

Published on 2024-08-31 15:34

Metaculus is a prediction market and forecasting platform that allows users to make predictions on various topics, including science, technology, politics, and more.

The Metaculus Q3 AI Forecasting Benchmark Tournament aims to compare LLM bot automated forecasters with crowds of human forecasters. 276 yes/no questions have been asked so far out of about 500 for the quarter. Bot forecasting primarily means, provided with some news (a popular choice being AskNews), to throw the news and the question at an LLM like Claude or ChatGPT and ask it to cogitate and make a prediction. As with all things, it's all in the asking: How should the LLM be prompted to answer? There are many different kinds of questions. It is tempting to group these questions by model domain, by which I mean the quantitative modelling field applicable to the question. Here are some examples of model domains in Metaculus (summary provided by Meta Llama 3.1-405B):

Meta.Science and Technology

  • Artificial Intelligence: Predictions about AI developments, such as when a certain AI milestone will be achieved or when AI will surpass human intelligence in a specific domain.

  • Biotechnology: Predictions about advancements in biotech, such as when a new medical treatment will be approved or when a certain disease will be cured.

  • Climate and Environment: Predictions about climate change, renewable energy, and environmental policies.

  • Computer Science: Predictions about developments in computer science, such as when a new programming language will be released or when a certain algorithm will be improved.

Politics and Economics

  • US Politics: Predictions about US elections, policy decisions, and other political events.

  • Global Politics: Predictions about international relations, global conflicts, and diplomatic developments.

  • Economics: Predictions about economic indicators, such as GDP growth, inflation rates, and unemployment rates.

  • Finance: Predictions about stock markets, cryptocurrencies, and other financial instruments.

Culture and Society

  • Entertainment: Predictions about movie releases, awards shows, and other entertainment events.

  • Sports: Predictions about sports tournaments, championships, and player performance.

  • Social Movements: Predictions about social justice movements, protests, and cultural trends.

Other Domains

  • Astronomy and Space: Predictions about space exploration, astronomical discoveries, and the search for extraterrestrial life.

  • Philosophy and Ethics: Predictions about philosophical debates, ethical dilemmas, and the impact of technology on society.

  • Miscellaneous: Predictions about unexpected events, unusual phenomena, and other topics that don't fit into other domains.

These model domains are not exhaustive, and Metaculus often adds new domains and topics as they become relevant and interesting to the community.

In the wider "superforecasting" community (in French, the super-collectif), there are prescriptions given to individuals in a crowed of forecasters on how best to formulate a prediction. These prescriptions typically go by catchy acronyms like CHAMPS KNOW, shown here:

This prescription can be boiled down into a one-size-fits-all generic prompt for an LLM that tells it how to systematically consider a question before coming up with a prediction. For example, this forecast by the HSeldon bot follows a clear pattern most likely prompted in an outline to the bot:

However, for a question like "will the strongest geomagnetic storm have a K-index ("Kp") greater than 5 and less than or equal to 6?", it is hard to imagine that anything but the "M" in "CHAMPS KNOW" will apply. In this case it is tempting to ask the LLMs to do the work of slotting the space of questions into specific model domains, at which point my temptation would be to then write model domain-specific prompts that detail exactly what are the best-practice or most appropriate models to apply, and how to apply them for the problem at hand. Not that I don't trust LLMs to just spitball the right approach automatically in a single shot, but a little good guidance never hurts.

So I'm giving several LLMs the following prompt "Please group the following questions by applicable mathematical model, and follow this up by summarizing the details of how each model applies." Claude: I'll group the questions by applicable mathematical model and then summarize how each model applies:

1. Bernoulli Model (Binary Outcome):

This model applies to questions with yes/no outcomes or events that either occur or don't occur.

Examples:

- Will Donald Trump debate the Democratic nominee for president before October 1, 2024?

- Will Apple announce a portless iPhone before October 1, 2024?

- Will Russia detonate a nuclear weapon in Ukraine or Ukrainian-held territory before October 1, 2024?

Summary: The Bernoulli model is used for events with two possible outcomes, typically success (1) or failure (0). It's characterized by a single parameter p, which represents the probability of success. This model is applicable to many of the questions that ask if a specific event will happen before a certain date.

2. Poisson Model:

This model is suitable for questions involving count data or the number of occurrences of an event in a fixed interval of time or space.

Examples:

- Will at least 5 major hurricanes occur in the 2024 Atlantic hurricane season before Sept 30, 2024?

- Will at least 24 world records be broken at the 2024 Paris Olympics?

- Will more than 10 Chinese aircraft violate Taiwan's air defense identification zone (ADIZ) in a single day between August 15 and September 15, 2024?

Summary: The Poisson model is used for discrete count data, especially for rare events occurring in a fixed interval. It's characterized by a single parameter λ, which represents both the mean and variance of the distribution. This model is useful for questions about the number of occurrences of specific events within a given timeframe.

3. Normal Distribution:

This model is appropriate for continuous variables that are symmetrically distributed around a mean.

Examples:

- Will the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U) exceed 316.000 for August 2024, according to FRED?

- Will the Canadian Dollars to U.S. Dollar Spot Exchange Rate exceed 1.3600 on Friday September 20, 2024, according to FRED?

- Will the time gap between the first and second place finishers in the 2024 Tour de France be more than 3 minutes?

Summary: The Normal distribution is used for continuous data that is symmetrically distributed around a mean. It's characterized by two parameters: μ (mean) and σ (standard deviation). This model is applicable to questions involving economic indicators, exchange rates, and other continuous measurements that tend to be normally distributed.

4. Multinomial Model:

This model is suitable for questions with multiple possible outcomes, where only one outcome can occur.

Examples:

- Will the Fed cut rates by 25 bps, 50 bps, more than 50 bps, or hold rates steady at their September meeting?

- Will USA, Sweden, England, France, or another country win the 2024 Warhammer 40,000 World Team Championship?

- Will Tadej Pogačar, Jonas Vingegaard, or someone else win the Tour de France 2024?

Summary: The Multinomial model extends the Bernoulli model to situations with more than two possible outcomes. It's characterized by parameters p1, p2, ..., pk, representing the probabilities of each outcome. This model is useful for questions where there are multiple mutually exclusive possibilities.

5. Time Series Models:

These models are appropriate for questions involving data points collected over time, often used for forecasting.

Examples:

- Will the S&P 500 index go up in September 2024?

- Will the Real-time Sahm Rule Recession Indicator increase Year-over-Year for Aug 2024?

- Will the CBOE S&P 500 3-Month Volatility Index (VXVCLS) be higher Year-over-Year on July 20, 2024?

Summary: Time Series models analyze data points collected at regular time intervals. They can incorporate trends, seasonal patterns, and other time-dependent factors. These models are useful for questions about financial markets, economic indicators, and other time-dependent phenomena.

6. Logistic Regression:

This model is suitable for questions where the outcome depends on multiple factors or predictors.

Examples:

- Will Benjamin Netanyahu remain Prime Minister of Israel through Sept 30, 2024?

- Will Ukraine withdraw from Kursk before October 1, 2024?

- Will the bubble in the Magnificent Seven pop before September 30, 2024?

Summary: Logistic Regression is used when the outcome is binary (yes/no) but depends on multiple predictor variables. It models the probability of an event occurring as a function of various factors. This model is useful for questions where the outcome might depend on complex, interrelated factors.

These models provide frameworks for analyzing and predicting the outcomes of the various questions, based on their specific characteristics and the nature of the data involved.

(End of Claude)

Claude:

You're absolutely right, and I apologize for not delving into more specific models for some of the specialized questions. Indeed, there are more tailored models for specific phenomena like geomagnetic storms and epidemics. Let's look at some of these:

1. Geomagnetic Storm K-index Model:

For questions like:

"Between August 21 and Sept 11, 2024, will the strongest geomagnetic storm have a K-index ("Kp") greater than 6 and less than or equal to 7?"

The K-index (Kp) is a specific measure used in space weather. While it's often represented as a quasi-logarithmic scale from 0 to 9, more sophisticated models are used for prediction:

- Coupled Magnetosphere-Ionosphere-Thermosphere (CMIT) models: These integrate solar wind parameters, ionospheric conditions, and magnetospheric dynamics.

- Artificial Neural Network (ANN) models: These have been developed to predict Kp values based on solar wind parameters.

- Autoregressive models: These use past Kp values and solar wind data to forecast future Kp indices.

These models often incorporate data from solar observations, satellite measurements of the solar wind, and ground-based magnetometer readings.

2. Epidemic Models:

For questions like:

"Will the 7-day average confirmed mpox cases in 2024 exceed the peak 7-day average confirmed mpox cases from the 2022-2023 outbreak by September 30, 2024?"

Epidemiological models are more appropriate:

- SIR (Susceptible, Infected, Recovered) Model: This basic model divides the population into three compartments and uses differential equations to model the flow between these compartments.

- SEIR (Susceptible, Exposed, Infected, Recovered) Model: An extension of SIR that includes an 'Exposed' state for diseases with incubation periods.

- Agent-Based Models: These simulate the actions and interactions of autonomous agents to assess their effects on the system as a whole.

- Network Models: These take into account the structure of contact networks in disease spread.

For mpox specifically, researchers have used variations of these models, often incorporating factors like sexual networks and vaccination strategies.

3. Avian Influenza Models:

For questions about H5N1 in humans or dairy cows:

- Spatial-temporal models: These account for both geographic spread and time progression of the virus.

- Host-pathogen interaction models: These focus on the dynamics between the virus and different host species (birds, humans, cows).

- Phylodynamic models: These combine genetic data with epidemiological models to track viral evolution and spread.

4. Climate and Weather Models:

For questions about hurricanes or air quality:

- Numerical Weather Prediction (NWP) models: These use current weather conditions as input into mathematical models of the atmosphere to predict future weather.

- Statistical models: These use historical data to predict future patterns, often in combination with NWP models.

- For Air Quality Index predictions, chemical transport models are often used, which simulate the emission, transport, mixing, and chemical transformation of trace gases and aerosols.

5. Financial and Economic Models:

For questions about market capitalization, exchange rates, or economic indicators:

- ARIMA (Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average) models: Often used for time series forecasting in finance.

- GARCH (Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity) models: Used for modeling volatility in financial markets.

- Econometric models: These combine economic theory with statistical methods to analyze economic data.

These specialized models provide more accurate and context-specific predictions for these particular types of questions. They incorporate domain knowledge and specific data types relevant to each field, allowing for more nuanced and precise forecasting than general statistical distributions alone.

(End of Claude)
