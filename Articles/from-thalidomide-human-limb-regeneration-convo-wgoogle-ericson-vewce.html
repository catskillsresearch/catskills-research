Lars: how exactly did thalidomide cause birth defects

AI on Google Search: Thalidomide causes birth defects by causing the degradation of key proteins and by inhibiting the growth of new blood vessels.

1. Disruption of Developmental Proteins The drug binds to a protein called Cereblon (CRBN), which marks other proteins for destruction.

  • SALL4 Degradation: Thalidomide forces Cereblon to target and degrade SALL4, a transcription factor essential for limb and internal organ development.

  • Signaling Interruption: This process disrupts signaling pathways, such as the FGF (Fibroblast Growth Factor) and Sonic hedgehog (Shh) pathways, which provide instructions for limb outgrowth and patterning.

2. Inhibition of Blood Vessel Growth (Antiangiogenesis) Thalidomide prevents the formation of new blood vessels.

  • Targeting Immature Vessels: In a developing embryo, limb buds are highly angiogenic. Thalidomide destroys these newly forming, immature blood vessels while leaving stable, mature vessels intact.

  • Cell Death: The loss of blood supply leads to cell death (apoptosis) in the limb buds, causing limbs to be shortened or absent.

3. Oxidative Stress The drug also increases the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative stress. This stress damages DNA and cellular structures in the embryo, contributing to developmental failures.

Critical Timing and Susceptibility The damage only occurs during a specific "critical period"—roughly 20 to 36 days after conception.

  • Specificity: The exact defect depends on which organs were forming when the drug was taken. For example, exposure on day 24 typically affects the arms, while exposure up to day 28 affects the legs.

  • Dosage: Even a single dose taken during this window is sufficient to cause severe malformations.

Lars: is there any way you could trick body into regrowing missing parts based on that mechanism of action

AI on Google Search: While researchers have identified the specific mechanisms thalidomide uses to stop limb growth, "tricking" the human body into regrowing limbs by reversing these mechanisms remains a theoretical dream.

Experimental efforts to manipulate these pathways for regeneration have yielded the following insights:

1. The "Permissive" vs. "Instructive" Barrier Thalidomide's primary target is SALL4, a protein that maintains cells in an "undifferentiated" state, allowing them to turn into limb parts.

  • Regeneration in Non-Mammals: In highly regenerative animals like the axolotl, SALL4 is naturally re-activated after injury to help form a blastema (a mass of stem-like cells).

  • Mammalian Limitations: Studies in mice show that overexpressing SALL4 in adult tissue is not enough to trigger limb regrowth. SALL4 acts as a "permissive" factor—it allows growth to happen but doesn't "instruct" the body to rebuild a limb.

2. Developmental "One-Way" Street The embryonic development thalidomide disrupts is a carefully timed "symphony".

  • Genetic Clock: Human limb development occurs during a strict window (20–36 days after conception). Once this window closes, the body essentially "loses the instructions" to replay that developmental program.

  • Fibrosis over Regeneration: In humans, adult wounds typically heal through fibrosis (scarring) to seal the injury quickly, whereas regeneration requires the wound to stay open and form a blastema.

3. Therapeutic Potential in Other Areas While we can't regrow limbs yet, scientists are "tricking" the body in other ways using these mechanisms:

  • Cancer Treatment: New drugs called PROTACs (Proteolysis-targeting chimeras) use thalidomide's "molecular glue" ability to selectively destroy cancer-causing proteins.

  • Organ Repair: Combining SALL4 with other proteins might help "reprogram" scar tissue in the heart into functional muscle, showing promise for cardiac regeneration after a heart attack.

Lars: where is the developmental program encoded

AI on Google Search: The "developmental program" is encoded through a system involving genetic blueprints, epigenetic software, and spatial signaling.

1. The Genetic "Hard Drive" (DNA) The fundamental instructions are written in DNA, specifically within highly conserved gene families.

  • Hox Genes: These provide positional identity, telling cells whether they are part of a shoulder, an elbow, or a hand. Humans have 39 of these genes organized into four clusters; for example, HOXA13 and HOXD13 are essential for developing hands and feet.

  • Transcription Factors: Genes like Tbx5 (for arms) and Tbx4 (for legs) act as "master switches" that trigger the specific program for that limb type.

2. The Epigenetic "Software" (Chromatin) The developmental program is "run" by turning specific genes on or off at the right time. This is managed by the epigenome.

  • Chemical Tags: Molecules like methyl groups attach to DNA to "silence" genes that aren't needed once a limb is finished.

  • Chromatin Folding: The DNA is physically packed so that only the "pages" relevant to limb growth are accessible during that specific 20-36 day window. After this, the "book" is effectively locked.

3. The Spatial "GPS" (Signaling Centers) The program also exists as a dynamic map of chemical gradients in the embryo.

  • Signaling Centers: Specific groups of cells, like the Apical Ectodermal Ridge (AER), act as command centers. They secrete growth factors (like FGFs) that create a "field" of information.

  • Positional Information: A cell "knows" where it is and what to become based on the concentration of these chemicals it senses.

4. Cellular Memory Some cells retain a "memory" of their original location. Research shows that fibroblasts (connective tissue cells) in different parts of an adult body still express different Hox genes corresponding to their original developmental position. This "positional memory" is a key area of study for future regenerative medicine.

  • The Result: This master switch tells the cell, "You are now at the limb site." The cell then starts producing its own signals (like FGF10), which physically pushes the skin out to form a limb bud.

  • Feedback Loops: The limb grows because two areas (the tip and the base) talk to each other using chemicals (FGF and SHH). As the limb gets longer, these two areas physically move too far apart for their signals to reach one another.

  • The Shutdown: When the signal drops below a certain level, the "Growth" program stops, and the "Differentiate" program (turning soft tissue into bone) takes over.

  • If/Then Logic: If Gene A is on AND Signal B is present, THEN Gene C (build bone) turns on.

  • Thalidomide's Role: It essentially "snips a wire" in this logic board by destroying SALL4, one of the critical "AND" gates. The clock keeps ticking, but the instructions for "build arm" are never received, so the program skips straight to "build hand" or "stop."

Lars: Could you unlock or get the same effect some way in an adult?

AI on Google Search: Unlocking the developmental program in an adult is a primary goal of regenerative medicine, but currently, we can only achieve "the same effect" in limited, experimental ways.

Researchers are exploring several ways to "re-open the book":

1. Partial Reprogramming (The "Yamanaka" Method) This involves using a set of proteins called Yamanaka factors (Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc).

  • The Goal: By briefly exposing adult cells to these factors, scientists can "partially reprogram" them. This loosens the epigenetic locks without fully erasing the cell's identity.

  • The Result: In mice, this has been used to rejuvenate aged liver tissue and promote muscle repair. However, if the "treatment" lasts too long, it can cause the cells to become cancerous.

2. Targeting the "Molecular Glue" Mechanism Scientists are designing PROTACs (Proteolysis-targeting chimeras) to selectively remove the "lock" proteins.

  • Specific Goal: Degrade the specific histone modifiers that keep limb genes silent, re-activate "dormant" developmental pathways.

3. Re-awakening "Positional Memory" Connective tissue cells (fibroblasts) remember where they are in your body.

  • The Discovery: By stimulating specific genes (like SP8), scientists can push adult mammalian cells to start forming a blastema.

  • The Current Limit: The "instructive" part of the program that tells them how to finish building a whole complex limb hasn't been mastered.

  • Mammalian Testing: His company, Morphoceuticals, is now testing this approach in mice.

  • Bioelectric Blueprint: Levin argues that we just need to send the right electrical signal to the "cellular network" to tell it to run the "build a limb" program again.

3. "Ancient" Genetic Switches (Texas A&M & Wake Forest) In April 2026, researchers at Texas A&M and Wake Forest University announced they had successfully regenerated skeletal and connective tissue in mammals.

  • Shared Circuitry: By comparing axolotls, zebrafish, and mice, they identified a shared "genetic program" for regeneration that exists in humans but is currently "locked".

  • The Breakthrough: They used a viral gene therapy to "wake up" this ability in mice, accelerating the regrowth of severed digits.
