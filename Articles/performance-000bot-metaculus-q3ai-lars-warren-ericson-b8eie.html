I enjoyed participating in the Metaculus Q3 AI Benchmarking Tournament. My bot, doing business as 000_bot, notched 40th place out of 55, putting it 7 seats out of the money, but not the worst of the lot, after making 383 forecasts on a diverse set of topics including politics, war, economics and markets, sports and science over a 3 month period:

A select set of high performing human forecasters was measured against each bot. The human forecasters were not expected to answer all 383 questions. The bots generally were: Not answering a question would imply a strategic decision to avoid which most likely requires human judgment. Bots were not allowed to have a human-in-the-loop process. So on the subset of questions that both humans and bots answered under similar circumstances (each question could only be forecast on the one day that it was live), Metaculus showed just 2 out of the 55 bots outperforming the humans...and those bots were not at the top of the Leaderboard for the entire set of questions. So how to slice and dice a bot-human performance comparison is not an obvious matter.

The distribution of peer scores shows that 000_bot's median score was slightly above the crowd-of-bots but with a much heavier left tail than right tail, which dragged it below the in-the-money bubble:

This heavy left tail is due to a feature of the Metaculus scoring system that trashed the scores of a lot of bots, namely a very heavy exponential penalty for overconfidence, defined as getting very near 1% or 99% on a forecast while also being wrong (all the questions in Q3 had a Yes/No outcome).

How do we explain why mf-bot-3 is 40th on the Bot leaderboard, but 1st on the Head-to-head leaderboard? One reason is this question about “Rockstar” winning a music award, where the bot median was 62% but mf-bot-3 forecast 1%. The question resolved Yes resulting in a -383 Peer score for mf-bot-3. However, the Pros did not forecast on this question, so it is not included in the Head-to-head leaderboard calculation.

The overall design of 000_bot is shown in this picture. We have N questions arriving each day. Often these are logically related groups, so the N questions will be worked on in M ≤ N groups:

The steps that 000_bot takes are as follows:

  1. Look for model domain/applicable quantitative methods for the question

  2. Look for questions in the batch grouped on the same topic to make sure probabilities are consistent

  3. Use a good news source with full articles (AskNews for this challenge) and share the same news set to a whole question group

  4. Use a generator-critic method to revise the forecast in a loop, looping until generator and critic agree within some tolerance on the forecast

  5. Repeat the generator-critic loop a number of  times to produce multiple forecasts (LLMs have a random component so each repeat of the process may find a different number) and average the probabilities to avoid overconfident outliers, which are heavily penalized on Metaculus as noted above and summarize the rationales of separate forecasts into a common rationale.
