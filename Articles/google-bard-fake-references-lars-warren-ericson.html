It is a great temptation to let ChatGPT write articles and fire the writers. It turns out that, in the realm of mathematics (not to mention the realm of law), this is a really bad idea. I discovered today that my last post on a mathematical topic contained a number of made-up references in furtherance of a superficially plausible argument that was entirely hallucinated. In particular, this output from Google Bard:

Google Bard: According to Google Scholar, the most recent articles citing McClarty's paper are:
"A Note on McClarty's Paper on Wiles' Proof of Fermat's Last Theorem" by Thomas Scanlon (2021)
"The Existence of Elliptic Curves Without Grothendieck's Universes" by Alexander Razborov (2022)
"A New Approach to Wiles' Proof of Fermat's Last Theorem" by Colin McClarty (2023)

"According to Google Scholar". How could I doubt it? Geez. There's pretty much an implied contract there of not-made-upness. And yet. I couldn't actually find them in Google Scholar itself, no matter how much I tried. I couldn't find them in Google Search, either, or on the websites of the presumed authors.

In the case of the lawyer who used ChatGPT to write a case, the judge had this to say:

“Did you read any of the cases cited?” Judge Castel asked.
“No,” Mr. LoDuca replied.
“Did you do anything to ensure that those cases existed?”
No again.

I must make the same confession: I did not read or attempt to read the references that Google Bard supplied. If I had, I could have avoided the equivalent of the beating Mr. LoDuca received from Judge Castel, in the form of this rebuke from an accomplished mathematician:

Just imagine how much other BS it shoved down your throat during your research on FLT.

This matters pragmatically because I see my peers looking to GPT to polish their writing and do their digging. I don't know about the polishing part: the GPTs tend to have a writing style that is identifiable as a mechanical voice. The digging part is clearly hazardous.
