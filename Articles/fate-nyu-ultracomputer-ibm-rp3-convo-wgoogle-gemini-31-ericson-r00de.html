The IBM RP3 (Research Parallel Processor Prototype) was an ambitious, experimental supercomputer project developed in the 1980s by IBM 's T.J. Watson Research Center in collaboration with the NYU Courant Institute School of Mathematics, Computing, and Data Science Ultracomputer project. It was heavily focused on researching the viability of highly scalable shared-memory and distributed-memory parallel computing.

Here are the answers to your specific questions:

Whatever happened to it?

The RP3 was never intended to be a commercial product, but rather a pure research vehicle heavily funded by IBM and Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) . While it was initially designed to scale up to 512 processors, only a single 64-node prototype—referred to as the RP3x—was actually built.

The prototype was fully operational from October 1988 through March 1991. It was used extensively by researchers (running a heavily modified version of the Mach operating system) to study cache coherency, memory contention, and parallel OS scheduling. After serving its purpose, the project was concluded. The hardware itself was retired, but the lessons learned directly influenced operating system design (such as Mach's SMP capabilities) and IBM's later successful commercial parallel supercomputers, such as the SP1 and SP2.

What was the geometry of the connection switch?

The RP3 architecture actually used two separate multistage interconnection networks operating in parallel:

  1. Low-Latency Network: This handled standard memory requests. It used the geometry of a rectangular SW Banyan network, which is similar to an Omega network but provides dual source-sink paths to minimize latency and route around congestion.

  2. Combining Network: This handled interprocessor coordination (synchronization). It was constructed using the geometry of Lawrie's Omega network. For the 64-port prototype, it was built out of six levels of 2x2 switches, supplemented by 4x2 and 2x4 concentrators and deconcentrators to handle the routing into the network.

How many CPUs and how much RAM?

  • CPUs: The architecture was specified to scale up to 512 processors, but the finalized functional prototype contained exactly 64 CPUs.

  • RAM: In the physical 64-processor prototype, each Processor-Memory Element (PME) contained 8 Megabytes of primary memory and a 32 KB (later upgraded to 64 KB) cache. This gave the 64-way prototype a total of 512 MB of RAM. (Had the full 512-node version been built, it would have featured between 1 to 2 Gigabytes of RAM). A key feature of the RP3 was that the memory could be dynamically partitioned by the software to act as local private memory, global shared memory, or a mix of both.

What CPU did they use?

The RP3 utilized IBM's 32-bit ROMP (Research Office Products Division Microprocessor). This was an early commercial RISC processor design that was also used concurrently in the IBM RT PC workstation. In the RP3, the ROMP was paired with a dedicated floating-point coprocessor.

Was Fetch&Add fully implemented in the switch nodes?

Yes, but with a slight architectural nuance.

The Fetch&Add primitive was the marquee feature inherited from the NYU Ultracomputer design. It was fully implemented in hardware, but the logic was divided between the switch nodes and the memory controllers:

  • The custom NMOS logic inside the 2x2 nodes of the combining network handled the "combining" phase. If multiple processors issued a Fetch&Add to the same memory address simultaneously (e.g., waiting on a hot-spot lock), the switch nodes would catch the collision, queue the requests, mathematically combine them on the fly, and send only a single aggregated request forward to the memory.

  • The memory controller at the destination executed the actual final addition as a single atomic operation.

  • As the response traveled backward, the switch nodes distributed the appropriate individual prefixed values back to each requesting CPU.

While the hardware logic was fully functional, putting arithmetic logic units inside the switches made the combining network incredibly complex and slow. The transit time of the combining network was several times greater than that of the standard low-latency network. Ultimately, the RP3 proved that hardware combining networks worked, but it also demonstrated that they were too expensive and complex for the modest performance benefits they provided, pushing the industry to favor software-based synchronization algorithms and cache-coherency protocols moving forward.

Part 1: The Network on the IBM SP1 and SP2

The Network Geometry: The IBM SP1 (introduced in 1993) and SP2 (1994) used a network called the High-Performance Switch (HPS). The HPS was based on the Vulcan switch chip, an 8x8 crossbar designed by IBM Research.

The geometry of the HPS was a Bidirectional Multistage Interconnection Network (BMIN). Unlike the RP3's one-way Omega network where data looped back around, the SP's bidirectional design allowed packets to traverse up the switch hierarchy just far enough to find a common ancestor to the destination node, and then travel back down. It utilized packet-switched, wormhole routing to keep latency very low.

Did they do Fetch&Add in the network? No, they did not. Unlike the RP3, the SP1 and SP2 were not shared-memory machines; they were distributed-memory multicomputers. Each node was a self-contained RS/6000 workstation with its own private CPU, RAM, and OS.

Because there was no global shared memory, processors could not natively issue a "Fetch&Add" instruction to another node's RAM over the switch. Instead, the SP switch was strictly used for message passing (running protocols like MPI, PVM, and IP). The Vulcan switch chips were simply high-speed data routers; they contained no Arithmetic Logic Units (ALUs) and did no mathematical combining of the packets passing through them.

Part 2: The Theoretical Limit (Scaling to 1024 nodes today)

If you were to build a 1024-node shared-memory system with modern technology, is doing Fetch&Add combining at the switch level still a bad idea?

The short answer is yes, for fine-grained shared memory, it is still a bad idea—but no, it is a brilliant idea for coarse-grained message passing (and is actually widely used today).

Here is how modern computer architecture views this problem:

Why it remains a bad idea for fine-grained Shared Memory (Fetch&Add)

If 1024 CPUs try to lock the same shared memory address simultaneously, you get a "hot spot" that can melt down a network. However, doing the combining in the transit switches is still avoided for three main reasons:

  1. The Cache Coherence Nightmare: Modern CPUs rely heavily on complex cache coherence protocols (MESI, MOESI) and directory-based coherence. If an intermediate network switch catches a memory request, mathematically alters it on the fly, and sends a combined answer back, it bypasses the strict cache coherence rules. Keeping the CPU caches synchronized when switches are secretly doing math on the data is incredibly difficult to engineer reliably.

  2. The "Fast Path" Latency Penalty: In modern VLSI, transistors are incredibly cheap, so putting an ALU in a switch is easy. The problem is latency. If you add inspection, queuing, and ALU logic into a transit switch, you slow down the routing pipeline. Modern systems prefer to keep the switch as "dumb and fast" as physically possible so standard memory reads/writes happen in nanoseconds.

  3. Better Alternatives Exist: Instead of doing atomics in the network, modern architectures (like modern x86, ARM, and GPUs) push the atomic logic directly to the Memory Controller or the Shared Last-Level Cache (L3/L4). The transit network just delivers the 1024 requests really fast. The memory controller receives them, pipelines them natively in hardware, and rapidly spits the results back out. When combined with smart software algorithms—like MCS locks or hierarchical combining trees, where processors spin on local memory flags rather than hammering a global address—the hot-spot problem is solved without complicating the network switches.

The Plot Twist: Switch-Level Combining is HUGE in Modern HPC

While the RP3's method of combining single-memory-address Fetch&Add instructions died out, the broader concept of in-network computing was actually decades ahead of its time.

Today, switch-level combining has returned triumphantly, just at a different level of the software stack. If you look at massive AI clusters or top-tier supercomputers today (scaling to thousands of nodes), they heavily rely on switch-level combining for Message Passing Collectives:

  • NVIDIA / Mellanox InfiniBand SHARP: Modern InfiniBand switches feature Scalable Hierarchical Aggregation and Reduction Protocol (SHARP). When thousands of GPUs are training an AI model, they all need to sum up their mathematical gradients (an MPI_Allreduce operation). Instead of sending all that data to the CPUs to be added up, the InfiniBand switches intercept the data, mathematically sum the numbers on the fly inside the switch, and broadcast the final result back to the GPUs.

  • Programmable Switches (P4): Modern network chips (like the Barefoot Tofino) allow engineers to program ALUs directly into the switch data plane to track states, count packets, or aggregate data at line rate.

Summary: The RP3 engineers were right about the physics of scaling—you do need the network hardware to help reduce data at massive scales. But history proved that it is better to use switch-level ALUs for large mathematical data reductions (message passing/AI workloads) rather than using it to manage simple memory locks (shared memory Fetch&Add).

Lars: Is MPI_Allreduce a generalization of Fetch&Add?

Gemini:

While both involve combining data across multiple processors, no, MPI_Allreduce is not a direct generalization of Fetch&Add.

Mathematically and practically, they serve different purposes, have different semantics, and return different results. If you are looking for the MPI equivalent of a combined Fetch&Add, you are actually looking for MPI_Scan (Prefix Sum) or MPI_Fetch_and_op (One-Sided Communication).

Here is a breakdown of why they are fundamentally different, and how they relate:

1. The Result: Unique Prefix vs. Global Total

The most critical difference is what the processor gets back.

  • Fetch&Add gives a unique result to every caller. If Processors A, B, and C simultaneously issue a Fetch&Add(1) to a memory location that holds 0, the network (like the RP3's combining switch) serializes them. Processor A gets 0, B gets 1, C gets 2, and the memory is updated to 3. This makes it perfect for synchronization—every processor gets a unique ticket to access an array or a queue without stepping on each other's toes.

  • MPI_Allreduce gives the exact same result to every caller. If A, B, and C each submit the value 1 into an MPI_Allreduce(SUM), they all get back 3. Nobody gets a 0, 1, or 2. This makes it perfect for data reduction and computation (like summing up the global error rate in a neural network), but totally useless for handing out unique queue tickets.

2. Synchronization: Asynchronous vs. Collective

  • Fetch&Add is completely asynchronous. A single processor can issue a Fetch&Add whenever it wants. It does not need to know what the other processors are doing, and the other processors don't even need to be awake. It just acts upon a single, shared memory address.

  • MPI_Allreduce is a collective operation. Every single processor in the defined communicator group must actively call the MPI_Allreduce function. If one processor doesn't call it, the entire application will hang (deadlock) waiting for it to participate.

3. Granularity: Pointers vs. Vectors

  • Fetch&Add operates on a single memory word (e.g., 32-bit or 64-bit integer) at a specific physical address.

  • MPI_Allreduce operates on arrays and vectors. You can pass it an array of 10 million floating-point numbers, and it will perform 10 million element-wise additions across all nodes simultaneously.

What is the equivalent of Fetch&Add in MPI?

If you want to achieve what the RP3's Fetch&Add combining network did, you would use one of these two MPI functions:

1. MPI_Scan (or MPI_Exscan) MPI_Scan performs a Prefix Reduction. If Processors A, B, and C each input the value 1, MPI_Scan will return 1 to A, 2 to B, and 3 to C. This perfectly mimics the "unique ticket" mathematical combining behavior of the RP3's switch, but in a synchronous, message-passing context.

2. MPI_Fetch_and_op Introduced in MPI-3, this is part of MPI's One-Sided Communication (Remote Memory Access or RMA) interface. It is the literal, direct software equivalent to a hardware Fetch&Add. It allows Processor A to asynchronously reach over the network into Processor B's memory, add a value, and return the old value, without Processor B's CPU being involved.

The connection to the previous answer

Why does it feel like they are related? Because in terms of hardware history, the infrastructure used to accelerate them is identical.

When the RP3 designers built ALUs into their switches to combine multiple Fetch&Add collisions, they were inventing in-network computing. Today, when modern network engineers put ALUs into InfiniBand switches to accelerate MPI_Allreduce, they are using the exact same architectural philosophy. The workload shifted from fine-grained memory locks to coarse-grained AI/HPC vector math, but the concept of "doing math in the network switch to save time" is a direct spiritual successor to the RP3.

Gemini:

Does anybody really need Fetch&Add?

Yes, absolutely. While it is not used in every program, it is a foundational building block for specific types of parallel algorithms.

In distributed-memory programming (and shared-memory multithreading), Fetch&Add (or its cousin, Compare_and_swap) is strictly required to implement lock-free data structures, distributed counters, and dynamic load balancing.

For example, if you are processing a massive, irregular graph (like a social network or an AI knowledge graph), work cannot be evenly divided up front. Instead, processors use a shared distributed counter. When a processor needs more work, it issues a Fetch&Add(1) to the global counter to grab a unique block of work. Without an atomic Fetch&Add, you would have to use a "master" process to hand out work, which quickly becomes a massive traffic bottleneck at scale.

How often is MPI_Fetch_and_op used in practice?

In the grand scheme of High-Performance Computing (HPC), it is used very rarely.

MPI_Fetch_and_op is part of MPI's One-Sided Communication (RMA) interface introduced in MPI-3. The vast majority of legacy and modern supercomputing codes (like fluid dynamics, weather modeling, and molecular dynamics) use strict, synchronous grid-based math. These codes rely heavily on Point-to-Point (Send/Recv) and Collective (Allreduce/Bcast) operations.

RMA operations—including MPI_Fetch_and_op—are highly specialized. Unless an application is written specifically using the PGAS (Partitioned Global Address Space) model or requires asynchronous graph traversal, programmers generally avoid it. In global HPC workloads, it represents a tiny fraction of a percent of total MPI calls.

Do they keep statistics on all the MPI operations?

Yes, they do. Massive supercomputing centers like NERSC, ALCF, OLCF, and European HPC centers run continuous, lightweight profiling on almost every job that goes through their systems.

They use specialized profiling tools—most notably Darshan (developed by Argonne National Lab), mpiP, and IPM. These tools intercept MPI calls at runtime to log how many times a function was called, the message sizes, and how much time was spent waiting. Centers aggregate this data over millions of jobs to understand global workload characteristics, find system bottlenecks, and tell the MPI Forum which functions they need to optimize in the hardware.

How many MPI operations are there?

The MPI standard is notoriously large, and it has ballooned significantly over the past 30 years. The API is generated by combining orthogonal concepts (e.g., a "Send" can be Blocking, Non-Blocking, Persistent, Ready, Buffered, or Synchronous—meaning "Send" alone accounts for over a dozen distinct functions).

Here is how the standard has grown:

  • MPI-1.1 (1995): ~130 functions (The basics: Send, Recv, Collectives)

  • MPI-2.0 (1997): ~290 functions (Added Parallel I/O and early One-Sided RMA)

  • MPI-3.1 (2015): 412 functions (Added Non-blocking Collectives, Neighborhood Collectives, improved RMA)

  • MPI-4.0 (2021): 467 functions (Added Persistent Collectives, partitioned communication)

  • MPI-4.1 (2023): ~500+ functions (Added hardware resource queries and "large count" _c function variants to support arrays larger than 2 billion elements).

Despite the massive size of the API, most developers only use the "Core 6" to write functional parallel programs: MPI_Init, MPI_Comm_size, MPI_Comm_rank, MPI_Send, MPI_Recv, and MPI_Finalize. Add in MPI_Allreduce and MPI_Isend/Irecv, and you cover 95% of all code written in HPC.

If they deleted MPI_Fetch_and_op, would anybody care?

Yes, a small but highly influential group of developers would care deeply.

If you view the MPI community as a pyramid, the 99% of general scientists at the bottom writing standard grid-based fluid dynamics or weather models would not notice its disappearance at all.

However, the 1% at the top of the pyramid—the developers who write the underlying high-performance libraries, frameworks, and next-generation Exascale applications—would protest loudly. Here is who would care:

  • PGAS Framework Developers: Languages and models like Global Arrays, Chapel, or OpenSHMEM rely entirely on One-Sided/RMA operations to function. They use MPI as a backend, and they need hardware-accelerated atomics.

  • Graph Analytics / AI Developers: Traversing massive, unstructured data (like social network graphs) is highly irregular. You can't use synchronous Send/Recv or Allreduce. They rely on Fetch_and_op to mark visited nodes or claim tasks dynamically.

  • Dynamic Load-Balancers: In advanced physics simulations (like tracking particles moving unpredictably between simulation zones), work imbalances happen quickly. Applications use Fetch_and_op on a distributed counter to implement "work stealing," allowing idle processors to dynamically grab work from a shared queue.

Low usage statistics do not mean low value. MPI_Fetch_and_op is a specialized surgical tool; it is rarely used, but when you are doing the specific surgery that requires it, nothing else works as well.

Are there workarounds?

If the MPI Forum deleted MPI_Fetch_and_op tomorrow, applications would not break permanently, but their performance and code complexity would suffer. Programmers would have to use one of the following four workarounds, ranging from easy-but-slow to functionally identical.

Workaround 1: The Direct Alternative (MPI_Get_accumulate)

  • How it works: MPI_Get_accumulate is essentially the "big brother" of Fetch_and_op. While Fetch_and_op performs an atomic operation on a single element (like one integer), Get_accumulate performs atomic operations on arrays/vectors. If Fetch_and_op were deleted, developers would just call Get_accumulate and set the array length to 1.

  • The Catch: It requires a slightly heavier function signature and sometimes incurs a tiny software overhead inside the MPI implementation, but it is functionally identical. In fact, Fetch_and_op was mostly added to the standard as a convenience function because single-element atomics are so common.

Workaround 2: The CAS Loop (MPI_Compare_and_swap)

  • How it works: If you want to safely add 1 to a remote variable without Fetch_and_op, you can use a Compare-And-Swap (CAS) loop. First, read the remote value (e.g., it's 5). Second, locally calculate the new value (5 + 1 = 6). Third, send an MPI_Compare_and_swap saying: "If the remote value is still 5, change it to 6." Finally, if another processor changed it to 6 while you were doing the math, your CAS fails. You read the new value and try again.

  • The Catch: CAS loops over a high-latency network are notorious performance killers. If 1,000 nodes try to increment a counter via CAS simultaneously, the network will be saturated with failed retries.

Workaround 3: Explicit Window Locks (MPI_Win_lock / MPI_Win_unlock)

  • How it works: You put a strict lock on the remote memory space using MPI's one-sided synchronization mechanics. You lock the memory, use a standard MPI_Get to read the value, add 1 locally, use MPI_Put to write it back, and unlock the memory.

  • The Catch: This completely ruins performance. Modern Network Interface Cards (NICs), like Mellanox ConnectX/InfiniBand, have hardware-level ALUs that can execute a Fetch_and_op natively on the network card in nanoseconds without waking up the target CPU. Using explicit software locks forces the system to do heavy synchronization, bypassing hardware acceleration.

Workaround 4: The Old-School Manager-Worker Model (MPI-1 fallback)

  • How it works: If you completely abandon MPI's One-Sided (RMA) features, you have to dedicate one entire processor (a "Manager" rank) to act as the counter. Every other processor sends a standard MPI_Send message requesting a ticket. The Manager rank runs a while loop, receives the message, increments a local variable, and replies with MPI_Recv.

  • The Catch: The Manager rank becomes a massive bottleneck. The application loses an entire CPU core just to hold a variable, and all parallel processors are forced to wait in a serialized line to get their counter value.

Summary: If MPI_Fetch_and_op were removed, developers would immediately default to Workaround 1 (MPI_Get_accumulate). However, the MPI Forum keeps Fetch_and_op in the standard specifically because modern network hardware provides native, ultra-fast support for single-element atomics, and having a dedicated MPI function allows compilers and network drivers to map the code directly to the bare metal.

It translates to dedicated hardware—but exactly where that hardware lives depends on the interconnect.

In modern High-Performance Computing (HPC), if you call MPI_Fetch_and_op over a top-tier interconnect (like InfiniBand, NVLink, or HPE Slingshot), the MPI library maps it directly to native hardware silicon. The target CPU is completely bypassed, and there are no software workarounds.

However, if you run that same MPI code on standard hardware (like basic Ethernet or older network cards), the MPI library detects the lack of hardware support and secretly drops down to a software workaround.

Here is exactly how it works "under the hood" for different technologies:

1. The Hardware Path: InfiniBand (Nvidia/Mellanox ConnectX)

If you are running across a modern supercomputer network via InfiniBand, MPI_Fetch_and_op translates directly into a hardware-level RDMA (Remote Direct Memory Access) command.

  • The Hardware Verb: The MPI library translates the call into a specific InfiniBand network instruction called IBV_WR_ATOMIC_FETCH_AND_ADD.

  • The Execution: The Network Interface Card (NIC) creates a special network packet and shoots it across the switch. When the packet arrives at the destination NIC, the destination CPU does not wake up.

  • The PCIe Atomic: The destination NIC uses a feature built into the PCIe bus (called PCIe Atomics) to send a hardware signal directly to the destination computer's memory controller. The memory controller's physical ALU does the math, writes the new value to RAM, and sends the old value back to the NIC, which sends it back over the network.

2. The Hardware Path: NVLink (GPU to GPU)

You specifically mentioned NVLink. If you are doing an atomic operation from one Nvidia GPU to another GPU in the same chassis, MPI_Fetch_and_op (or its CUDA equivalent) maps directly to NVLink silicon.

  • NVLink is essentially a highly specialized memory bus. The sending GPU executes an atomic instruction that is routed directly over the NVLink cables to the receiving GPU.

  • The receiving GPU routes that request straight into its L2 Cache and Memory Controller partition.

  • Nvidia GPUs have dedicated raster operations/atomic arithmetic units sitting right next to the L2 cache lines specifically designed to do this math in hardware. The target GPU’s streaming multiprocessors (the main "cores") are completely uninterrupted.

3. The Hardware Path: HPE Slingshot (Exascale Supercomputers)

On the newest supercomputers (like the Exascale Frontier machine), the network is HPE Slingshot. The Slingshot NICs (code-named Rosetta) have dedicated, highly tuned hardware engines designed exactly for PGAS languages and one-sided MPI.

  • These NICs actually have their own internal hardware caches and ALUs. When they receive an atomic request, the NIC itself can manage the atomic lock, do the math, and update the host memory, ensuring microsecond latency.

What happens when you DON'T have the hardware? (The Workarounds)

If you try to run MPI_Fetch_and_op on standard Gigabit Ethernet, a laptop, or over a network that doesn't support RDMA Atomics, the underlying MPI implementation (like OpenMPI or MPICH) acts as a translator and falls back to a software workaround.

Under the hood, OpenMPI will secretly spawn an "Active Message" system.

  1. Your process sends a standard network message saying, "Please add 1 to memory address 0x1234."

  2. On the target machine, a hidden background thread (often called an async progress thread) provided by the MPI library receives this message.

  3. That background thread wakes up the target CPU.

  4. The target CPU takes out a local software lock, reads the memory, adds 1, saves it, and sends a standard network message back with the result.

Why this distinction matters

The difference between the hardware execution and the software workaround is night and day.

  • Dedicated Hardware (InfiniBand/NVLink/PCIe): The operation takes 1 to 3 microseconds, operates at millions of operations per second, and has zero impact on the target node's computation.

  • Software Workaround (Active Messages): The operation takes 10 to 50+ microseconds, creates massive operating system jitter, ruins the target CPU's cache, and destroys parallel scaling.

This is exactly why high-end interconnects are so expensive. When you buy a $2,000 Mellanox ConnectX-7 NIC or an NVSwitch, you aren't just buying bandwidth—you are buying the dedicated silicon ALUs that intercept operations like MPI_Fetch_and_op and execute them entirely in hardware.

Did Blue Gene die out?

Yes, the Blue Gene architecture completely died out. The final generation of the architecture was Blue Gene/Q (such as the Sequoia supercomputer at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory), deployed around 2011–2012. After that, IBM canceled plans for the successor (often rumored as Blue Gene/W) and retired the lineage.

It didn't die because it was a bad design; it died because the fundamental physics and economics of the semiconductor industry experienced a massive paradigm shift.

Blue Gene was based on homogeneous, lightweight multicore scaling—packing millions of relatively weak, low-power embedded processors (PowerPC) onto a single machine. However, around 2010–2015, the industry shifted to heterogeneous computing (accelerators). Instead of millions of weak CPUs, it became far more power-efficient to build "Fat Nodes" containing a few highly powerful CPUs paired with massively parallel GPUs (like NVIDIA and AMD).

IBM adapted to this shift by abandoning Blue Gene and partnering with Nvidia to build systems like Summit and Sierra (2018), which used IBM POWER9 CPUs paired with Nvidia GPUs. By the 2020s, IBM largely stepped back from building the hardware for the world's absolute largest supercomputers, yielding the floor to HPE (Cray), Nvidia, and AMD.

Do 2026 models have Blue Gene's take on hardware combining?

Conceptually, yes. Physically, no.

Blue Gene's brilliant "take" on hardware combining was to build a physically separate network. On Blue Gene/L, if you wanted to send a normal message, it traveled over the 3D Torus network. If you called MPI_Allreduce to sum up a massive array of math, it was routed onto a completely separate, physically wired Global Tree Combining Network whose sole purpose was to do math on the fly.

In 2026, we still do hardware combining, but we no longer build separate physical networks for it.

Instead, we use In-Network Computing (INC) on unified networks. The silicon inside modern primary switches has become incredibly dense and programmable.

  • InfiniBand SHARP: On massive modern Nvidia-based AI clusters (using Quantum/ConnectX InfiniBand), a technology called SHARP (Scalable Hierarchical Aggregation and Reduction Protocol) is used. The main network switches intercept the MPI or AI reduction packets on the primary data lines, dynamically build a "virtual tree" inside the switch logic, mathematically combine the data in the switch's internal ALU, and send the result back.

  • HPE Slingshot: Exascale machines like Frontier and El Capitan use HPE Slingshot Ethernet switches. These switches have dedicated, integrated arithmetic engines that do exact same hardware combining over standard data cables.

Essentially, modern engineering figured out how to cram Blue Gene's dedicated Global Tree network directly into the silicon of the standard high-speed data switch.

1. Inside the Processor (CPU ↔ GPU ↔ RAM)

2. Inside the Compute Node (APU ↔ APU)

An individual compute node in El Capitan (specifically, the HPE Cray EX255a blade) contains four MI300A APUs sharing a single board.

  • The Bandwidth: This mesh provides 384 GB/s of peer-to-peer data transfer per APU, allowing the four APUs (and their combined 512 GB of HBM3 RAM) to act as one massive, unified NUMA domain.

3. Where the HPE Slingshot Nodes Live (Node ↔ Node)

Here is exactly where Slingshot physically fits into the architecture:

The HPE Slingshot NICs (codenamed Cassini) plug directly into these PCIe Gen 5 lanes. This gives the network card direct, high-bandwidth access to the APU's memory without passing through a separate host processor.

  • The Slingshot Switches: The NICs route copper or optical cables out to the massive HPE Slingshot network switches (powered by the Rosetta switch ASIC).

  • The Network Topology: The Slingshot switches interconnect the 11,000+ nodes of El Capitan using a Dragonfly topology.

  • - In a Dragonfly topology, groups of cabinets are wired together into high-density local "groups" (where every switch connects to every other switch).

A "crossbar of crossbars" perfectly describes a Fat-Tree (or Clos network), which is what older systems like the IBM Blue Gene/Summit or modern standard AI clusters use.

The Dragonfly topology was specifically invented to replace the "crossbar of crossbars." It is more accurately described as a "Fully Connected Mesh of Fully Connected Meshes."

Here is the fundamental difference, and why Dragonfly is the standard for Exascale supercomputers like El Capitan:

1. The "Crossbar of Crossbars" (Fat-Tree)

In a Fat-Tree, you have a hierarchy.

  • You have "Leaf" switches at the bottom connected to the CPUs.

  • You have "Spine" (or Core) switches at the top.

  • If Node A wants to talk to Node B in another cabinet, the data goes up from the leaf to the central spine crossbar, and then down to the other leaf.

  • The Problem: As a supercomputer scales to 10,000+ nodes, you need massive, centralized "core" switches that do nothing but route traffic. Millions of incredibly expensive, long, optical cables must be run from the compute racks to these central core racks.

2. The Dragonfly Topology (Mesh of Meshes)

The brilliant innovation of the Dragonfly topology (first proposed in 2008 by John Kim, William Dally, and others) is that it eliminates the core switches completely. There is no "top" of the network. Every single switch in the entire network is attached to compute nodes.

Instead of routing traffic up to a central hub, the long-distance optical cables are distributed evenly across the local switches.

Here is how it is built:

Level 1: The Intra-Group (The Local Mesh) Inside a row of cabinets, you take about 64 to 96 switches and wire them together so that every switch connects to every other switch in that group. This is a local all-to-all mesh. Electrical copper cables are mostly used here because the switches are physically close to each other, which saves a massive amount of money.

Level 2: The Inter-Group (The Global Mesh) Now you have dozens of these highly dense "Groups" spread across the data center. Instead of running cables from these groups up to a central crossbar, the groups act as virtual mega-switches.

  • The system is wired so that Group 1 has exactly one long-distance optical cable connecting it to Group 2, one connecting it to Group 3, one to Group 4, and so on.

  • Group 2 has one cable to Group 1, Group 3, Group 4, etc.

  • Every Group is connected directly to every other Group.

The Result: The "3-Hop" Rule

Because of this "mesh of meshes" geometry, any compute node in the entire El Capitan data center can reach any other compute node in a maximum of exactly three network switch hops.

Let’s trace a packet from Node A in Group 1, to Node B in Group 99:

  1. Hop 1 (Local): Node A sends the data to its local switch. The local switch looks at the destination and says, "I don't have the long-distance cable to Group 99, but my neighbor Switch 5 in this cabinet does." It sends the packet over a cheap copper wire to Switch 5.

  2. Hop 2 (Global): Switch 5 fires the packet across the data center over its dedicated optical cable directly to Group 99. It lands on Switch 42 in Group 99.

  3. Hop 3 (Local): Switch 42 in Group 99 says, "I received the packet, but Node B is attached to Switch 12 in my group." It forwards the packet over a local copper wire to Switch 12, which drops it directly into Node B.

Why did HPE Slingshot use this for Exascale?

Cost and Latency. Optical transceivers (the lasers that drive fiber optic cables) are one of the most expensive and power-hungry components of a modern supercomputer.

A "crossbar of crossbars" (Fat-Tree) requires almost every connection to go over long optical cables to the core. A Dragonfly topology localizes traffic, maximizing the use of cheap, low-power copper wires inside the groups, and mathematically minimizes the absolute number of expensive optical cables required to connect the rest of the room—all while guaranteeing that maximum 3-hop latency.

Lars: Briefly sketch the NYU Ultracomputer project in 1983. It is N processors, N memory units, and N log N 2x2 switches, where the switch can combine memory references between processors to the same address using an instruction called "Fetch&Add". Elaborate.

Gemini:

In 1983, the NYU Ultracomputer project, led by Allan Gottlieb, Jack Schwartz, and their team at the Courant Institute, published their landmark architectural design. The goal was to build a massively parallel, shared-memory MIMD (Multiple Instruction, Multiple Data) computer that could scale to thousands of processors without choking on memory contention or synchronization locks.

To solve the scaling problem, they envisioned an elegant, highly theoretical hardware architecture:

1. The Topology

The system consisted of $N$ processors on one side and $N$ memory modules on the other. Connecting them was an Omega network (a specific type of multistage interconnection network). To connect $N$ processors to $N$ memories, the network required $\log_2(N)$ stages, with each stage containing $N/2$ switches. This meant the total number of switches scaled at roughly $O(N \log N)$.

The fundamental building block of this network was a custom 2x2 switch node that contained memory buffers, ALUs (Arithmetic Logic Units), and routing logic.

2. The "Fetch&Add" Instruction

In traditional computing, if 1,000 processors try to grab a ticket from a shared queue counter, they have to wait in line. One processor locks the memory, reads it, adds 1, writes it back, and unlocks it. The other 999 wait. This serialization destroys parallel performance.

The Ultracomputer introduced a hardware-level atomic primitive called Fetch&Add (F&A). The instruction is written as F&A(Address, Value). When a processor executes it, it indivisibly returns the old value at that memory address, and updates the memory by adding the new value to it.

3. The Elaboration: Hardware Combining at the Switch

The true genius of the 1983 Ultracomputer design was that the Fetch&Add operation was not executed at the memory module alone; it was executed dynamically inside the $N \log N$ routing switches.

Here is how the hardware combined references:

  • The Collision: Imagine Processor 1 ($P_1$) issues F&A(X, 1) and Processor 2 ($P_2$) simultaneously issues F&A(X, 2) to the exact same memory address ($X$).

  • The Interception: Both requests enter the network and happen to arrive at the input ports of the same 2x2 switch at the same time.

  • The Combine (Forward): Instead of forcing one request to wait, the switch’s internal ALU intercepts them. It realizes they are targeting the same address. The switch mathematically adds the two values together ($1 + 2 = 3$). It then stores $P_1$​'s value ($1$) in its local hardware buffer, and forwards a single, combined request of F&A(X, 3) to the next stage of the network.

  • The Memory Action: The memory module receives only one request: F&A(X, 3). Let's say the original value of $X$ was 10. The memory updates $X$ to 13, and returns the original value (10) back down the wire.

  • The De-combine (Backward): The return message (10) arrives back at the 2x2 switch. The switch remembers that it combined $P_1$ and $P_2$. It uses its local buffer to distribute the answers:

  • - It gives 10 back to $P_1$.

  • - It takes 10, adds $P_1$​'s buffered value (1), and gives 11 back to $P_2$.

The Result

Both processors receive a valid, unique result (10 and 11), exactly as if they had executed sequentially in a random order. However, they executed in parallel, in the exact same amount of time it would take a single processor to access memory.

If 1,000 processors issued a Fetch&Add to the same address, the $N \log N$ network would form a temporary cascading tree, combining requests at every switch stage. Only a single request would ever hit the memory module, and the network switches would perfectly distribute 1,000 unique queue tickets on the way back down.

In 1983, this concept was revolutionary. It completely eliminated the "hot-spot" memory contention problem, allowing for theoretically perfect scaling of parallel operating system queues and loops.

Here are the details of who built it, how they did it, and the ultimate success of the project:

Who did that?

The VLSI design of the Ultraswitch was spearheaded by a dedicated hardware team at NYU's Courant Institute. The key figures were:

  • Susan Dickey: The lead VLSI designer for the project (who later wrote her Ph.D. dissertation on combining switch architectures).

  • Richard Kenner: The lead hardware engineer. (Fun historical note: Kenner later became highly famous in the software world as the primary maintainer of the GNU C Compiler [GCC] in the early 1990s).

  • Marc Snir and Jon Solworth: Computer scientists who helped conceptualize the underlying routing algorithms and switch architecture, co-authoring the foundational 1984 paper, "The Ultraswitch—a VLSI Network Node for Parallel Processing."

How was it built?

To make hardware combining work, the switch couldn't just be a dumb set of wires; it had to be a "smart" router with integrated math logic.

The team designed custom silicon that featured semi-systolic queues and associative wait buffers. When a memory request entered the switch, a hardware comparator checked to see if an identical memory address request was sitting in the queue. If it found a match, the switch's internal ALU mathematically added the values, sent the combined request forward, and held the local state in the associative wait buffer until the memory responded.

They fabricated the chips using the MOSIS service (a DARPA/NSF-funded program that allowed universities to print custom silicon). The final working iterations were fabricated using a 1.6-micron double-metal CMOS process.

With what success?

The VLSI implementation was a tremendous academic and functional success, but it hit a harsh physical ceiling.

The Successes:

  1. It actually worked. Unlike many academic architectures that never leave the chalkboard, the NYU team successfully fabricated the chips and built a fully functional 16-processor, 16-memory module prototype.

  2. It proved the math. The VLSI chips successfully executed pairwise (2-way) combining of Fetch&Add instructions on the fly, proving that Jack Schwartz's theory of a "hot-spot-free" shared-memory network was physically possible.

  3. Pioneering In-Network Compute: It was one of the first successful demonstrations of putting active computational ALUs directly into the data-routing plane of a multistage network.

The Limitations: While the 16-node machine was a success, the VLSI effort revealed the severe physical limitations of 1980s silicon:

  • Pin and Area Limits: A 2x2 switch doing pairwise combining was right at the limit of what could fit on a chip. To scale the Ultracomputer efficiently to thousands of nodes, researchers calculated that the switches would need 3-way or 4-way combining. Dickey and Kenner noted that scaling the VLSI layout to handle 3-way combining resulted in multi-input adders that took up too much silicon area and severely slowed down the clock speed.

  • Latency: The logic required to check the queues, compare addresses, and do the math took several clock cycles. This meant that even when the network was empty and no combining was required, standard memory reads were heavily penalized by the "smart" switch's transit latency.

Ultimately, the NYU VLSI effort successfully brought the Ultraswitch to life. It proved that hardware combining was a brilliant solution to memory contention, but it also proved that the transistor budgets of the 1980s were simply not large enough to make it commercially viable against standard, "dumb-but-fast" memory networks.
